Part 1 · covers the two designs and the six edits

A copy cannot hear the edit

6 min live

Design A types the client's name and rate into every project, task and invoice when the row is created. Design B keeps them once, in a Clients row, and every other row holds a relation to it. Both look identical on day one. The difference only appears when something changes, so the bench changes things: twelve realistic edits in date order, six applied by default.

LiveThree counts, and what "the truth" means3 min▶

Rows disagreeing with the truth counts the 158 projects, tasks and invoices whose client label no longer names the client's current record. Invoices billed at a stale rate counts invoices whose rate is not the contract rate in force in the week they were issued, with the dollars billed off contract beside it. Questions answered wrong asks five things every freelancer asks of her workspace: revenue by client this quarter, how many clients were billed this quarter, Brightwell's open tasks, total billed this quarter, and the rate the next invoice will use. Each is right or wrong against the truth.

"The truth" is each client's current name and the contract rate in force on an invoice's week. That is a choice, and a fair one: an invoice sent before a rename may legitimately carry the old name on its PDF, but the label your database groups by has to identify the client you have now, or every by-client total splits.

Self-studyThe edits, the habits, and what is modelled3 min▶

The default six edits, in order: week 5, Corvid Studio's rate goes from $95 to $110; week 7, Aldermoor Health is renamed Aldermoor Care; week 9, a duplicate client called "Brightwell", created by mistake in week 3, is merged into Brightwell Mutual; week 12, Aldermoor's rate goes from $120 to $135; week 15, Dunmore Council becomes Dunmore District Council; week 18, Brightwell's rate goes from $125 to $140. The slider adds six more, ending with a rename and a rate change in week 25.

Modelled habits: on design A, Ines copies the current name and rate into a row when she creates it, and invoices copy the rate from their project. Her monthly sync (rung 2) runs at the end of every fourth week and touches only what her views show her: active projects, open tasks and unpaid invoices, where an invoice counts as paid four weeks after issue. Everything else is counted on the rows.

Part 2 · covers what each rung fixes, and what it does not

Names, rates and totals are three different problems

6 min live

These are the numbers the widget prints at six edits, written down so they can be argued with after class. The Python reference reproduces every one.

LiveRungs 1 and 2: a clean-up chases a moving target2 min▶

On copied text, six edits leave 52 of 158 rows disagreeing and 3 of 5 questions wrong. A monthly find-and-replace roughly halves the drift to 27 rows and leaves 3 stale invoices where copied text left 7, but it can only fix what Ines's views show her. Done tasks and paid invoices keep the old names forever, and every invoice issued between a rate change and the next sync goes out at the old rate. Drag the slider to 12: rung 2 climbs to 99 disagreeing rows and 4 wrong questions, because the edits keep coming and the sync only ever runs behind them.

LiveRung 3: the relation fixes names, not copied numbers2 min▶

With every row related to one Clients row, disagreeing rows drop to 0 the moment the duplicate is merged, and the count of clients billed is right. Yet 7 invoices are still billed at a stale rate, the same 7 as on rung 1, and revenue this quarter still shows $57,855 against $58,845. The rate was typed onto each project when it started, so it is still a copy; a relation only fixes the facts that are read through it. That is session 3's rule made exact: one fact, one place applies to every fact, not just the one you noticed.

Self-studyRung 4, and the rollup that would rewrite history3 min▶

On rung 4 the invoice reads the client's rate through a rollup when it is issued, and keeps that value: 0 disagreeing rows, 0 stale invoices, 0 wrong answers. The keeping matters. If the invoice's rate were a live rollup, it would follow every later rate change, and the widget counts what that would do: 14 past invoices would silently change amount. A rate in force when work was billed is its own fact, the billed rate, and it deserves its own place. Session 5 builds the button that freezes it.

One honest limit: at 0 edits every rung shows 21 disagreeing rows and 2 wrong questions, because the duplicate Brightwell row exists from week 3. No design finds a duplicate for you. A relation makes the merge one action instead of a hunt, which is why rungs 3 and 4 fall to 0 at edit 3.

Real world

The rebrand that split the revenue report. A common small-business story: a long-standing client changes its name, new invoices use the new one, and the year-end "revenue by client" view shows two half-size clients where there was one big one. Nobody made a mistake on any single row. The workspace simply had the name in more places than the rename reached. Rung 1 at six edits reproduces exactly that split.

Part 3 · covers the anti-lever and the break

One giant table, and the row that disappears

6 min live

Two moves feel like simplification. Putting everything in one table with every property feels tidy, and deleting a client you no longer work with feels like housekeeping. The bench runs both and prints what they cost.

LiveThe anti-lever: one giant table with every property3 min▶

Put clients, projects, tasks and invoices in one database with a Type column, and every row carries every property: 165 rows by 16 properties is 2,640 cells, and 1,519 of them, 57.5 percent, are empty, because a client has no due date and a task has no rate. The filters break next. A view meant for overdue tasks, "Due before today and Done unticked", also catches 37 invoices, which have a due date and no Done box to tick, beside the 15 tasks that really are overdue. Every view now needs an extra "Type is Task" filter, forever.

And the table fixes nothing about drift: client names are still typed text, so the counts are rung 1's, 52 disagreeing rows and 3 wrong questions. The honest nuance: a single database is not always wrong. Notion lets one database hold several data sources, and a small list of genuinely similar things belongs together. What fails is mixing different kinds of thing so that no property means the same on every row.

LiveThe break: delete the row everything points at2 min▶

Delete Brightwell Mutual, the client with the most revenue. On rungs 3 and 4, 50 rows lose their client: their relation now points at nothing, and $17,230 of this quarter's revenue (rung 3; $17,770 on rung 4) drops out of every by-client total, silently. On rungs 1 and 2 nothing is orphaned, because nothing was ever connected, yet the same 50 rows still name a client with no page, and nothing warns you. Neither design is safe from deletion. The relation design at least makes the damage visible as empty cells you can filter for.

What the bench models and Notion's help pages do not say: the bench treats the deleted row as gone. In Notion a deleted page goes to Trash and stays 30 days by default, and restoring it is the fix. What a relation displays while its target sits in Trash is not on the help pages read for this session, so try it in a test workspace before you trust either answer. For a client you no longer work with, set a Status of Past instead of deleting.

Archive with a property, never with the delete key. A Status of Past keeps every relation, every rollup and every old invoice intact, and one filter hides past clients from the views you use daily.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · On rung 3 every row relates to one Clients row, yet 7 invoices are still billed at a stale rate. Why?

The relation fixed every name, which is why disagreeing rows are 0. The rate was never read through the relation; it was copied onto projects, and copies drift. Rung 4 reads it from the client row at issue time.

2 · Why does rung 4 read the rate once, at issue, instead of using a live rollup of the client's current rate?

The rate billed on an invoice is its own fact, fixed when the work was billed. Rollups can sum and show numbers (Notion lists Show original, Sum and others), but a live one would make history change whenever a rate does.

3 · The giant-table anti-lever shows 57.5 percent empty cells and an overdue view that catches 37 invoices. What is the root cause?

165 rows is small. The trouble is that a client has no due date and an invoice has no Done box, so most cells are empty and a filter on Due and Done catches invoices. Separate databases keep each property meaningful on every row.
